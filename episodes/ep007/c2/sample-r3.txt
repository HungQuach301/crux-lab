Tessa's mortgage fallback costs $120 a month. She's borrowing $320,000, and in her city that payment is her biggest bill.

A 15-year mortgage, or a 30-year paid off in 15 years anyway, with its lower payment kept for a bad month? We replayed every week of US mortgage surveys to price that fallback in dollars, and what each extra dollar of principal earns.

Tessa is illustrative, but her rates are real: the survey for the week of October 8, 2026 has the 15-year at 6.73 percent and the 30-year at 7.4 percent. This is US only, and it's history, not a forecast. It won't say which loan to take, or whether to invest.

Here is what Tessa is weighing. On $320,000, the 15-year loan's payment is $2,828 a month. The 30-year loan only requires $2,216.

That gap, about $613 a month, is the fallback: in a tight month, it's the least the 30-year asks of her. The bigger payment fits her budget today, but only just, and a job change or a big repair could squeeze it. Still, she wants the house paid off in 15 years, so the 30-year only works for her if she pays it like a short loan.

At 7.4 percent, finishing that loan in 15 years takes $2,948 a month. That's $120 a month more than the 15-year loan, for the same house and the same 15 years. Over those 15 years, the extra adds up to $21,622.

In Tessa's world, that price is a stack of bills beside her house: the stack is what keeping the fallback costs. It exists because the 30-year carries a higher rate on the same balance: on October 8, 0.67 point higher.

Paid on its own 30-year schedule, her loan would charge $477,622 in interest. Paid in 15 years, it charges $210,691.

The 15-year loan charges $189,069, and the difference between those last two totals is exactly the stack. So the 30-year paid in 15 isn't the same loan as the 15-year: it's the 15-year plus a fallback, and the fallback has a price. Is Tessa's stack a normal price for that fallback, or is the week of October 8 unusual?

Many walk-throughs of this question use one assumed rate and treat paying early as the obvious win. Here, a real 15-year loan stands next to the 30-year paid in 15, at the real rates of every week since 1991. We priced the same $320,000 both ways in each of 1,833 weeks, at that week's own survey rates; how it was built is on screen and in the description. Tessa's week is the last of them, and in every earlier week we asked her question: what would the fallback have cost then?

The answer comes down to one thing: how far the 30-year rate sits above the 15-year. Since 1991, that gap has run from 0.2 point to 1.0 point.

It averaged 0.57 point, and was at least half a point in about 2 in 3 weeks. Tessa's gap on October 8 sits a little above that average.

Stand a stack beside every week, and the stacks rise and fall with that gap. The smallest was $6,097.

The tallest was $29,502.

The typical week, the median, priced the fallback at $16,631. Fewer than 1 in 5 weeks since 1991 put a price this high on it. That makes Tessa's fallback expensive by history. What a lower payment is worth in a bad month, this replay can't measure.

Line the weeks up by decade, and the pattern is not the one most people expect. In the 1990s, the typical fallback cost $15,118.

In the 2000s, with lower rates, it cost about the same: $14,210.

In the 2010s, rates were far lower, yet the fallback cost more: $20,374.

The reason is the gap: in the 2010s and 2020s it typically ran about 0.7 point, against about 0.45 in the 1990s and 2000s. A lower rate didn't mean a cheaper fallback; a wider gap meant a pricier one.

Since 2020, the typical week priced it at $21,345, close to Tessa's own stack. That's history, not a forecast: the gap has moved before, and nothing here says where it goes.

Back to Tessa's own loan, and a shortcut many people reach for: pay the 30-year exactly what the 15-year would cost, and assume it ends on the same day. At the October 8 rates, it doesn't: it ends after about 195 months, about 15 months late. To finish in 15 years, she'd add $733 a month on top of the required payment.

That extra never lowers what the loan requires: the required payment stays where it was, and the loan simply ends sooner. Each extra dollar does cut the next month's interest; the payment just stays fixed, so more of it goes to principal. That's why the fallback is still there years later, if a month goes wrong.

Tessa has another practical question: could she send the extra once a year instead of every month? At the October 8 rates, that's $8,792 once a year, the same total as the monthly extra. Paid that way, the loan ends four months later.

And it charges about $8,200 more in interest, because each dollar sits on the balance longer before it arrives. That isn't a verdict on paying yearly: the money stays in her account longer, and what it earns there isn't counted. Which leaves the question underneath all of this: when Tessa puts an extra dollar into the mortgage, what does it actually earn?

Every extra dollar of principal stops interest from being charged on that dollar, at the loan's own rate. On Tessa's 30-year, that's 7.4 percent a year, before taxes. It isn't an investment return; it's interest she no longer pays. That rate is the bar any other use of that dollar gets measured against, before taxes. Whether any investment clears it, once risk and taxes count, isn't tested here.

The bar moves with the rate a loan was taken at. Since 1991, it has stood as high as 9.25 percent.

And as low as 2.65 percent.

Only about 1 week in 5 had a bar as high as Tessa's.

Leo, also illustrative, took a 30-year loan of the same size in January 2021, the week of that lowest rate, and paid it like a 15-year too. His fallback was cheaper: $13,231 over 15 years.

And it left him more room: his required payment sat about $793 a month below the 15-year's. But his bar was 2.65 percent: each extra dollar he paid earned only that, before taxes. Same plan, a different week, and it means something else. For Leo, the stack was short and the bar was low; for Tessa, the stack is tall and the bar is high.

Any fixed-rate borrower can set their own pair beside hers. The bar is that loan's own rate, printed on the paperwork, and the stack depends on how far apart the 15-year and 30-year offers sit that week. Clearing the bar isn't the whole story: a dollar paid into the house is hard to get back in a bad month, which is exactly what the fallback is for.
