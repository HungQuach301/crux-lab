## Summary

The video asks how much lower a variable private-loan rate must start, compared with a fixed rate, before the risk of a variable rate has been worth it. It tests this on an illustrative $50,000, 10-year loan (9% fixed vs 7.5% variable). It replays every 10-year stretch of US history since 1954, moving the rate with the 3-month Treasury bill. "Worth it" means how often the variable loan cost more total interest than the fixed loan, and by how much at worst. The answer depends on which era you land in. From 1981 on, a 2-point head start was enough every time. From 1954 to 1980, even 3 points was not: 10.5% of starts still cost more, the worst by $6,033. The 1.5-point head start in the example lost in 14.2% of all stretches (28.4% in the early era, 3.5% after 1981), and the worst case, April 1977, cost $11,219 more (43% more interest). To remember: a bigger head start helps but never removed the risk in a rising-rate era, and this is history, not a forecast. Real offers also differ on index, cap, fees and grace period.
