Students already enrolled and borrowing before July 2026 keep Grad PLUS for up to 3 more academic years, or for the time left in their program if that is shorter. New students like Leah still have the basic federal loan, a Direct Unsubsidized loan, up to $20,500 a year and $100,000 total, with higher limits in professional programs like medicine or law. When a program costs more than that after other aid, the rest can come from a private lender, and that's where Leah's two offers come from. Same lender, same amount, two kinds of rate. Both are for $50,000 over 10 years, illustrative numbers, not a real lender's offer. The variable loan's first payment is $593.51, about $40 less a month than the fixed $633.38. A fixed rate never changes, so neither does the payment. A variable rate follows a market interest rate, so the rate and the payment rise and fall with it, and nobody knows where it goes next. The distance between the two starting rates is what this video calls the head start. Leah's fixed rate is 9% and her variable rate starts at 7.5%, so her head start is 1.5 points. A point here means one percentage point of interest. Real offers differ: a head start can be bigger, smaller, zero, or negative, with the variable rate starting higher.

Here's how the replay works. A real variable rate follows the lender's own index, and the replay needs a much longer record than that index has. So it uses a stand-in with a long US record: the rate on 3-month Treasury bills. In August 2026, that rate was 3.72%, so Leah's 7.5% is that rate plus a fixed 3.78 points. Each month after that, her rate moves up or down exactly as much as the Treasury bill rate moved in the same month of history. If history would push the index below zero, the replay stops it at zero. The first replay starts her loan in January 1954 and runs it for 10 years, 120 monthly payments. Repayment starts right away, with no grace period, no fees and no rate cap. The replay adds up the interest and sets it next to the 9% fixed loan's. Then it starts again one month later, and again, up to September 2016, the last start with a full 10 years of data. Each start month becomes one token, sorted into the bin under the half of history where it began. That's 753 starting months. The stretches overlap, so they are not 753 independent tries, and the data is US only.

So how can both of those results be true? The answer is the head start, and when it does its work. Every month the variable rate sits below 9%, Leah pays less interest than on the fixed loan, and that saving builds a cushion. The cushion grows fastest at the start, when she owes the most, so every point of rate is charged on the biggest balance. When the rate later climbs above 9%, the extra interest has to use up that cushion before the variable loan has cost more overall. A short jump above 9% only dents it; emptying it needs rates that rise early and stay high for years. In a stretch where rates drift down, the cushion just keeps growing. And that is where the two halves of the record differ.

From 1954 to 1980, the Treasury bill rate mostly climbed, in waves. It peaked at 16.3% in May 1981, then mostly drifted down over the following decades, with big swings. Same loan, same head start; the only difference is where in history it began. Loans started in the climbing years often met rates that rose early and stayed high, and loans started after the peak mostly rode rates down. The best stretch began in August 1981, near that peak, and cost $15,295 less than the fixed loan. But across the 1954-to-1980 starts more than 1 in 4 cost more, against about 1 in 30 from 1981 on, and the worst, from April 1977, cost $11,219 more.

So, back to the question: how much lower does a variable rate have to start before the risk has been worth it? Measured the way this video measures it, how often it cost more in total interest than the 9% fixed loan and how much more at worst, history gives two answers. For starts from 1981 on, none cost more once the head start reached 2 points. For starts from 1954 to 1980, even 3 points left 10.5% costlier, the worst by $6,033. Which kind of history comes next is something no replay can show. Leah's illustrative offer sits at 1.5 points, short of the 2-point mark where none of the 1981-on starts cost more. At her point, more than 1 in 4 of the 1954-to-1980 starts and about 1 in 30 of the later ones still cost more, the worst by $11,219. A real offer has its own two rates, its own head start and its own place on the line. It also has what this replay leaves out: the lender's real index, a rate cap, a grace period, fees, and the borrower's own budget. The line doesn't say which offer is better; it shows what each head start meant in each kind of history. History, not a forecast.
