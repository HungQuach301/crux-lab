From July 1, 2026, new graduate students in the US can no longer borrow federal Grad PLUS loans. Leah, an illustrative student weeks from her first spring term, has two private offers on her laptop: 9% fixed, or variable starting lower, at 7.5%. How much lower does a variable rate have to start before that risk has been worth it?

Students already enrolled and borrowing before July 2026 keep Grad PLUS for up to 3 more academic years, or for the time left in their program if that is shorter. New students like Leah still have the basic federal loan, a Direct Unsubsidized loan, up to $20,500 a year and $100,000 total, with higher limits in professional programs like medicine or law. When a program costs more than that after other aid, the rest can come from a private lender, and that's where Leah's two offers come from. Same lender, same amount, two kinds of rate.

Both are for $50,000 over 10 years, illustrative numbers, not a real lender's offer. The variable loan's first payment is $593.51, about $40 less a month than the fixed $633.38. A fixed rate never changes, so neither does the payment. A variable rate follows a market interest rate, so the rate and the payment rise and fall with it, and nobody knows where it goes next. The distance between the two starting rates is what this video calls the head start. Leah's fixed rate is 9% and her variable rate starts at 7.5%, so her head start is 1.5 points. A point here means one percentage point of interest. Real offers differ: a head start can be bigger, smaller, zero, or negative, with the variable rate starting higher.

This video replays Leah's illustrative loan through every 10-year stretch of US interest rates since January 1954. "Worth it" gets one plain meaning here: how often the variable loan cost more in total interest than the 9% fixed loan, and how much more at worst. Then the replay moves the head start, from a variable rate 3 points under the fixed rate to one 1 point over it, to see where that history changes. By the end there's a line that any real offer can be held up against, including yours. This is history, not a forecast.

And the first result looks like a contradiction. Leah's variable rate rose above 9% at some point in about 3 in 4 stretches. Yet it cost more in total interest in about 1 in 7. That's more than 1 in 4 for starts from 1954 to 1980 and about 1 in 30 from 1981 on, and the worst stretch cost $11,219 more. The reason both are true is the head start.

Every month the variable rate sits below 9%, Leah pays less interest than on the fixed loan, and that saving builds a cushion. The cushion grows fastest at the start, when she owes the most, so every point of rate is charged on the biggest balance. When the rate later climbs above 9%, the extra interest has to use up that cushion before the variable loan has cost more overall. A short jump above 9% only dents it; emptying it needs rates that rise early and stay high for years. In a stretch where rates drift down, the cushion just keeps growing.

Here's how the replay works. A real variable rate is the lender's own index plus a fixed margin, and the replay needs a much longer record than that index has. So it uses a stand-in with a long US record, the rate on 3-month Treasury bills, and sets the margin so that Leah's rate starts at 7.5%. From then on, her rate rises and falls exactly as much as the Treasury bill rate did. The first replay starts in January 1954 and runs for 10 years, and the next starts one month later, and so on, up to September 2016. Each one is set against the 9% fixed loan over the same 10 years. Neighbouring stretches share most of their years, so they are not independent tries, and the data is US only.

And the two halves of the record are very different. From 1954 to 1980, the Treasury bill rate mostly climbed, in waves. It peaked at 16.3% in May 1981, then mostly drifted down over the following decades, with big swings. Same loan, same head start; the only difference is where in history it began. Loans started in the climbing years often met rates that rose early and stayed high, and loans started after the peak mostly rode rates down. The best stretch began in August 1981, near that peak, and cost $15,295 less than the fixed loan. But across the 1954-to-1980 starts more than 1 in 4 cost more, against about 1 in 30 from 1981 on, and the worst, from April 1977, cost $11,219 more.

Here is Leah's same loan, started in April 1977. It begins at 7.5%, like every replay, and for a while her cushion grows. Then the Treasury bill rate climbs year after year, and her rate follows it, to 19.3% at its highest. The cushion from her first months is soon gone, and every further month above 9% adds to what she owes in interest. Her payment reaches $863.36 a month, against the fixed $633.38. Over 10 years, the fixed loan charges $26,005 in interest. Leah's variable loan charges $11,219 more than that, 43% on top. This replay has no rate cap; a real loan's cap, if low enough, would have made this worst case smaller.

So far, every result has been for one pair of rates, 7.5% against 9%. So the replay ran again, holding the fixed rate at 9% and moving only where the variable rate starts. Each starting point is a different offer someone could be holding. At Leah's 1.5 points, 28.4% of the 1954-to-1980 starts and 3.5% of the later ones cost more, the worst by $11,219. At 2 points, none of the starts from 1981 on cost more, but 20.4% of the 1954-to-1980 starts still did, the worst by $9,472. At 3 points, still none from 1981 on, but 10.5% of the 1954-to-1980 starts cost more, the worst by $6,033.

With a smaller head start, or a variable rate that starts higher, more stretches cost more in both halves, and the worst gets bigger. At every head start tested, the worst stretch began in April 1977. And at every head start tested, some of the 1954-to-1980 starts still cost more. So, back to the question: how much lower does a variable rate have to start before the risk has been worth it? Measured as how often it cost more in total interest than the 9% fixed loan, and how much more at worst, history gives two answers. From 1981 on, a 2-point head start was enough every time; from 1954 to 1980, even 3 points was not, and the worst still cost $6,033 more. Which kind of history comes next is something no replay can show.

Leah's illustrative offer sits at 1.5 points, short of that 2-point mark, where stretches from both halves of history still cost more and the worst was $11,219. A real offer has its own two rates, its own head start and its own place on the line. It also has what this replay leaves out: the lender's real index, a rate cap, a grace period, fees, and the borrower's own budget. The line doesn't say which offer is better; it shows what each head start meant in each kind of history. History, not a forecast.
