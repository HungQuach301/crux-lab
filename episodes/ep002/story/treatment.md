# Episode 2 — Treatment (C2, WRITER, 2026-10-01)

Logline A (C1). Working title A1: *Need Private Grad Loans? Variable vs Fixed Through History*. Central question: **how much lower does a variable rate have to start than a fixed rate before the risk has been worth it, in history?** Loan numbers are ILLUSTRATIVE; the index is real US data. History, not a forecast.

## Context

One line of policy opens the film: starting July 1, 2026, a new graduate student can no longer borrow a federal Grad PLUS loan (`ctx_plus_end`). Then who it hits and who it doesn't: continuing students already enrolled and borrowing keep it for up to 3 more years (`ctx_plus_exception`); the basic Direct Unsubsidized loan remains, $20,500 a year and $100,000 total (`ctx_unsub_*`); professional programs have higher limits. Only when costs, after aid, run past that does the rest come from private lenders. Never "everyone now needs private loans."

## The person

**Leah** (illustrative; name for the owner to approve — alternatives Dana, Priya, Marcus), a new master's student weeks before her first spring term, has two offers on her laptop: 9% fixed, or variable starting at 7.5%. $50,000 over 10 years — said aloud and shown as illustrative. The concrete pull is the first bill: $593.51 against $633.38.

## The problem

A variable rate follows a market rate nobody can see ahead. The film defines **head start** in plain words before using it — "the distance between the two starting rates" — Leah's is 1.5 points, and real offers can be bigger, smaller, zero or negative. (Fixes the C1 stumble on "head start".)

## The journey

1. **The replay.** A stand-in index with a long record (3-month T-bill; SOFR is too young). 7.5% = 3.72% (August 2026) + 3.78 points; each month her rate moves as the T-bill moved in that month of history. A 10-year frame slides one month at a time from January 1954 to September 2016: 753 overlapping (not independent) stretches, US only. No cap, no grace period, no fees — spoken.
2. **The paradox, inside the film.** Bad news first: her rate rose above 9% in about 3 in 4 stretches; her payment passed $633.38 in more than half; one hit $863.36. Then the turn: in **total interest, against the 9% fixed loan**, the variable loan cost more in about 1 in 7 — in the same breath, more than 1 in 4 for 1954–1980 starts, about 1 in 30 from 1981 on, worst April 1977 at $11,219 more. The comparison is always named (fixes the second C1 stumble).
3. **Why both are true.** The head start fills a cushion, fastest when the balance is biggest; months above 9% must drain it. Short spikes dent it; only rates that rise early and stay high empty it.
4. **Two kinds of history.** Rates climbing in waves to 1980; peak 16.30% in May 1981; a long uneven drift down. Best stretch August 1981, median $3,832 less, worst from the first era.
5. **The worst stretch as Leah's loan.** April 1977: rate to 19.3%; $11,219 on top of the fixed loan's $26,005 interest — 43% more.

## The answer: the line

The 7.5/9 pair is one point on a line. Fixed held at 9%, only the head start moves, and every point is spoken with both periods and the worst case: 0, 1, 1.5, 2, 3 and −1 points. The answer comes in two halves: **from 1981 on, a 2-point head start was enough in every stretch; for 1954–1980 starts, even 3 points was not** (10.5% still costlier, worst $6,033). Which history comes next, no replay can show.

## Self-check close

Leah sits at 1.5 points, and she is illustrative. A real offer has its own two rates, head start and place on the line, plus what the replay leaves out (real index, cap, grace period, fees, budget). No advice, no forecast; the viewer leaves with a line to read their own offer against (G-013). "History, not a forecast." End-screen tail ~15–20 s.
