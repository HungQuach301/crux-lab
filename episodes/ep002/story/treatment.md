# Episode 2 — Treatment v3 (C2, WRITER, 2026-10-01)

Logline A (C1). Working title A1: *Need Private Grad Loans? Variable vs Fixed Through History*. Central question: **how much lower does a variable rate have to start than a fixed rate before the risk has been worth it, in history?** Loan numbers are ILLUSTRATIVE; the index is real US data. History, not a forecast.

## Context

One line of policy opens the film: starting July 1, 2026, a new graduate student can no longer borrow a federal Grad PLUS loan (`ctx_plus_end`). Then who it hits and who it doesn't: continuing students already enrolled and borrowing keep it for up to 3 more academic years or the time left in their program, whichever is shorter (`ctx_plus_exception`); the basic Direct Unsubsidized loan remains, $20,500 a year and $100,000 total (`ctx_unsub_*`); professional programs have higher limits. Only when costs, after aid, run past that does the rest come from private lenders. Never "everyone now needs private loans."

## The person

**Leah** (illustrative; name for the owner to approve — alternatives Dana, Priya, Marcus), a new master's student weeks from her first spring term, has two offers on her laptop: 9% fixed, or variable starting at 7.5%. $50,000 over 10 years, said aloud and shown as illustrative. The concrete pull is the first bill: $593.51, about $40 less a month than $633.38. Cold open: CO-A (dated context line, then Leah) by default; CO-B (Leah first) in `cold-open-options.md`.

## The problem

A variable rate follows a market rate and nobody knows where it goes next. The film defines **head start** in plain words before using it, "the distance between the two starting rates" (a point = one percentage point). Leah's is 1.5 points, and real offers can be bigger, smaller, zero or negative.

## The promise and the paradox (S03)

"Worth it" is defined once: how often the variable loan cost more in total interest than the 9% fixed loan, and how much more at worst. That definition is used again in the answer. The paradox now sits in the promise: her rate rose above 9% in about 3 in 4 stretches, yet it cost more in total in about 1 in 7. That is more than 1 in 4 for 1954–1980 starts and about 1 in 30 from 1981 on, and the worst cost $11,219 more. Then: the head start will move, a line is coming, and this is history, not a forecast.

## The journey

1. **Why both are true** (straight after the puzzle, v3). The cushion fills fastest when the balance is biggest. Months above 9% must drain it. Short jumps only dent it; falling stretches keep filling it.
2. **The replay.** The lender's index has too short a record, so the 3-month T-bill stands in. 7.5% = 3.72% (August 2026) + 3.78 points, and the rate moves month by month as the T-bill moved, with a zero floor. Frames run from January 1954 to September 2016: 753 overlapping stretches, the data US only; index value, margin, 753 starts, zero floor and no cap/grace/fees sit on the method card (v3). Each token drops into the bin under its half of history.
3. **Two kinds of history.** Rates climbed to 1980, peaked at 16.3% in May 1981, then drifted down. The best case (August 1981, $15,295 less) is always paired with both periods and the worst.
4. **The worst stretch as Leah's loan.** April 1977: the rate reaches 19.3% and the payment $863.36. She pays $11,219 on top of the fixed loan's $26,005, which is 43% more.

## The answer: the line

The 7.5/9 pair is one point on a line. The fixed rate is held at 9%. Spoken stops: 0, Leah's 1.5, 2 and 3, each with both periods and the worst case. The 1-point and −1 stops and all overall shares appear only on the slider. The worst always starts in April 1977, and the 1954–1980 share never falls to zero. The answer comes in two halves, using the "worth it" definition: **from 1981 on, a 2-point head start left none costlier; for 1954–1980 starts, even 3 points left 10.5% costlier (worst $6,033).** Which history comes next, no replay can show.

## Self-check close

Leah sits at 1.5 points, short of the 2-point mark that cleared every 1981-on start, with more than 1 in 4 of the 1954–1980 starts still costlier. She is illustrative. A real offer has its own two rates, head start and place on the line, plus what the replay leaves out (real index, cap, grace period, fees, budget). No advice, no forecast; a line to read any offer against (G-013). End-screen tail ~15–20 s.
