# Treatment — Episode 3 (C2 draft v1)

**Working title (C1, A1):** *Money You Won't Touch for 20 Years: Savings Bond or T-Bills?*
**Viewer:** an American with money set aside for about 20 years, now sitting in a bank account or rolling T-bills, weighing a Series EE bond that is guaranteed to double at 20 years.
**Spine (G-009):** context → character → problem → journey → answer. Numbers arrive only when the viewer needs them; every number is tied to Dana's money or to "your own 20 years" (G-008, G-013).

## Cold open — one person, one moment (G-007)
Dana, in her forties (ILLUSTRATIVE), has savings she has promised herself she won't touch for 20 years. Right now they roll from one 3-month Treasury bill to the next. Tonight she has a different tab open: a US savings bond whose promise is simple — held 20 years, it is worth at least double. Which path would have left her better off?

Before a single historical number, the rule of the episode, in plain words: the doubling promise in its current form only began with bonds issued in May 2005; earlier bonds had other terms; so for every year before that, we simply pretend today's promise already existed, and the screen marks it every time. Visually this is a long timeline, hatched and labelled "IF today's guarantee had existed" up to May 2005, with a short solid stretch after. That label rides on every pre-2005 result for the rest of the film.

## The two rules
The promise of the episode is stated next: how often history let the roll beat double, when it fell short, and where the line sits that your own 20 years would have to clear. Then the two rules, as two physical paths. The bond: 2.40 percent fixed for bonds issued May to October 2026, reset each May and November; topped up to double at 20 years, which works out to 3.53 percent a year; nothing extra before 20 years, and a three-month interest penalty before five. The bills: no promise past three months; 80 bills in 20 years, each at whatever rate the market sets; 3.72 percent in August 2026.

The trap is named, not walked into: today's 3.72 next to the bond's rate settles nothing — measured differently, and one month is not 20 years. What decides the race is the bill rate across all 20 years, which nobody knows in advance, us included. So we replay history.

## The journey — the replay
A 20-year roll from every start month, January 1934 to September 2006: 873 rolls, heavily overlapping, so really a few long stretches of history. Each finished roll drops as a dot onto a money-multiple axis with a fixed ×2 gate — the new "finish-line swarm".

**The answer:** the roll beat double 52.3 percent of the time, about half — but 93.1 percent for starts from 1950 to 1989, nearly always, and 5.0 percent from 1990 on, almost never (and never from 1934 to 1949). "About half" is separate eras stitched together. Then the only starts where the promise was real: 17 months, May 2005 to September 2006; none reached double (1.378 to 1.388 times). Small sample, one era — it shows what happened once, not what a guarantee does.

**Why the eras differ:** the path after the start. From 1990 on, the 20-year average bill rate ended below the starting rate 87.6 percent of the time; the starting rate has been a poor guide. Counterweight: not a reason to pick either path; it is what history did with these two rules.

**Double is about dollars:** against consumer prices, double kept its buying power in 58.7 percent of starts; the worst, January 1966, bought 58.0 percent of the original — for Dana, twice the dollars buying a little more than half. The bill roll faces the same prices (not scored here). No "doubling protects you".

## The answer for "someone like me" (G-013)
A line to measure your own 20 years against: the bill rate averaged 3.42 percent since 1934, and a roll needs to average 3.47 percent across all 20 years to beat double (the bond's doubling on the bill-rate basis; 3.53 is the same ×2 compounded yearly), a rule that matched all 873 starts; the middle roll ended at 2.097 times, barely over. The question isn't today's rate but where 80 bills average — which nobody can say. The line says what the roll must do, not which to pick.

Method in one sentence + V7 card. Close on Dana, same two paths: history doesn't choose for her. US only, and history, not a forecast. End screen.
