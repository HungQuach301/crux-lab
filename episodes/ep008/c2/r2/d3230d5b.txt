It's September 2026, and Nina pays rent again. Is her rent money wasted? She has saved enough for the down payment and the closing costs on a $400,000 home.

We replayed her choice in every month since 1991, one twin buying and one renting, to see whether renting cost more than buying over ten years, and when it didn't.

Nina and her twin are illustrative; the home prices, rents and mortgage rates are real. US only, and history, not a forecast. Nothing here says whether to buy or to keep renting. What it shows Nina is a whole street of answers, one house for each month.

So what does each twin actually pay? The twin who buys pays the down payment and the closing costs, then takes a 30-year fixed mortgage at that month's average rate. Every month after that, Nina's buying twin pays interest, property tax, insurance and repairs, and at the end, the cost of selling. Property tax, insurance and repairs come to 2.5 percent of the home's value a year. The twin who rents moves into an equivalent home and keeps the same savings.

Here is the part most people picture differently: the renting twin's savings sit in cash, not in stocks. That pile starts as the down payment and closing costs that never went into a house. Whatever renting saves each month is added to the same pile. In one variant, the cash earns Treasury bill interest, and nothing more. So whether investing the difference would have done better is a different question, and this episode doesn't answer it. A few costs are left out on both sides, and the card lists them.

Most rent or buy math picks one growth rate for prices and one for rents, then shows a single answer. It doesn't show how that answer moves when the assumptions move, and it doesn't replay what actually happened in the US. Here, prices, rents and mortgage rates come from the real monthly US record. One thing can't come from that record: the rent level the renting twin starts at.

So it hangs on the apartment door as a tag: a starting rent of 5 percent of the home's price a year. On a $400,000 home, that's $1,667 a month today, carried back through history with the government's rent index. The tag matters because the rent decides how much the renting twin sets aside each month, and so how big that cash pile grows. Later, the tag turns, and the answer turns with it.

So what does the real record say about the twins? Each start month gets one identical house on a long street, January 1991 on the left, July 2016 on the right. That's 307 houses, and each one is followed for ten years. Nina's own month sits past the end of the street, in the fog, because its ten years haven't happened. After ten years, a house lights up when the buying twin's equity, after the cost of selling, is bigger than the renting twin's cash. When the cash is bigger, its door goes dark.

So, walking the street from 1991, how often did the buying twin end up ahead? Through the 1990s, every house lights up. Prices climbed, the loan balance shrank, and by year ten the buyer's equity was ahead of the renter's cash, start after start.

Then, from April 2001, doors start going dark. Every house where the renting twin came out ahead sits between April 2001 and December 2007.

Of the 72 start months from 2002 through 2007, the buyer ended ahead in just 6. Those starts ran straight into the fall in national home prices.

Then the street lights up again: every start from 2009 to mid-2016 ended with the buyer ahead. Those buyers started from lower prices and lower mortgage rates.

Counted along the whole street, the picture is this. In about 3 in 4 of the 307 start months, the buyer ended ahead. In the other 75, the renter did. That count already includes interest, property tax, insurance, repairs, and the cost of buying and of selling.

That lit share is a count of history, not a promise for any one month. In the middle start month, the buyer ended about $157,000 ahead. But the spread around that middle is wide.

The brightest house is April 2012: ten years later, the buyer was about $484,000 ahead.

That loan started at 3.91 percent, and national prices more than doubled over the window. It's one month at the very top of the range, not a typical one.

And the darkest house is June 2006. Ten years on, the June 2006 buyer was $82,385 behind.

Beside that house stand a pair of stacks: the buyer's equity, $115,374, and the renter's cash, $197,760.

National prices rose only 3.2 percent in those ten years, while the buyer paid interest, tax, insurance and repairs the whole way. And that loan started at 6.68 percent. That's the national index, not one house; a single home in a single city could have done much better or much worse.

Does the length of the stay change the picture? Over five years instead of ten, the buyer ended ahead in 74.4 percent of start months.

The worst five-year start, May 2006, left the buyer $149,392 behind. Five years leaves little time to cover the cost of buying and then selling.

Over 15 years, every start month that history allows ended with the buyer ahead. Even the weakest of them left the buyer $79,829 ahead. From five years to ten to fifteen, the dark doors thin out, and then they're gone. Fifteen years is a long stay, and this street says nothing about how long Nina stays.

What made one house bright and another dark? A pair of illustrative neighbors on that street show what the month alone did. Marcus bought in June 2006, at the darkest house, and ten years later his renting twin was ahead. Priya bought in April 2012, at the brightest, and ended far ahead of hers. Same rent tag, same costs, same street; only the month was different. And on the day each of them bought, neither could see which house they were standing in.

So far, every house used the same tag: a starting rent of 5 percent of the price a year. A higher starting rent means a pricier rental, so the renting twin sets aside less each month. What happens to the street when the tag turns? At 4 percent, the buyer ended ahead in 64.2 percent of start months.

At 6 percent, 92.2 percent. The share moves with the rent you assume.
