Rent vs buy, replayed through every start month since 1991. In about 3 out of 4 of 307 start months, the twin who bought ended ten years ahead of the twin who rented, and the answer moved with the month and with the rent you assume.

Nina (ILLUSTRATIVE) rents in September 2026 and has saved enough for a 20% down payment. Her question: is her rent money wasted? We gave her a pair of twins in every month from January 1991 to July 2016. One buys a home priced at $400,000 (illustrative, the same price in every start month), paying the down payment and closing costs in dollars of the day and taking a 30-year fixed mortgage at that month's average rate; the other rents an equivalent home and keeps the same savings in cash. Ten years later we compare the buyer's equity, after the cost of selling, with the renter's cash.

The buyer ended ahead in 75.6% of the 307 start months. A dark stretch sits in the middle: of the 72 start months from 2002 through 2007, the buyer ended ahead in just 6; the June 2006 buyer was $82,385 behind. Held fifteen years, every start month that history allows ended with the buyer ahead. The share moves with the rent you assume: at a 4% rent tag the buyer ended ahead in 64.2% of start months, at 6% in 92.2%; if the renter's cash earns Treasury bill interest, the buyer's share falls to about 2 out of 3. Nina's own first month today: her assumed rent of $1,667 is 56.8% of the buying twin's first month, $2,933, at the September 2026 average rate of 6.86%.

Data through July 2026 (house prices), August 2026 (rents), October 8, 2026 (rates).
- Method: in every start month, the buyer pays 20% down and 3% closing costs, takes a 30-year fixed mortgage at that month's average rate, and pays interest plus property tax, insurance and repairs (2.5% of the home's value a year); at year ten the home is sold at a 6% cost of selling. The renter pays rent, keeps the down payment, the closing costs and whatever renting saves each month as cash, and does not invest it. All amounts are in dollars of the day (not adjusted for inflation).
- The one assumption the record cannot supply is the starting rent: a rent tag of 5% of the home's price a year today ($1,667 a month on $400,000), carried back through history with the rent index relative to the price index. The Census Housing Vacancy Survey puts the national figure at 4.83% and 5.59% a year in its two latest quarters, which give 73.6% and 87.3%: a reference, not Nina's own rent.
- The house price index covers many homes, not one house; a single home in a single city could have done much better or much worse. The rent index follows sitting tenants and can understate market rent growth.
- Not counted on either side: income taxes, the mortgage interest deduction, refinancing, moving costs, renter's insurance. Whether investing the renter's cash would have done better is a different question, not answered here.
- Nina, her twins, Marcus and Priya are ILLUSTRATIVE; the prices, rents and rates are real. Nina's first month compares first months only; it does not say how a 2026 start ends.
