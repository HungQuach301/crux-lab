In February 2026, the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate in the US fell to 5.98 percent, the lowest since September 2022.
Nora had borrowed in 2023, at 7.62 percent.
At the February rate, a new loan would have cut her payment by $459 a month.
She didn't take it.
By late September, the rate was back above 7 percent, for the first time since January 2025.
Now a letter comes from her lender: an offer to refinance, a new loan at a slightly lower rate that pays off the old one, for $5,124 in fees.
How big a rate cut makes that worth it for Nora — and where would your own loan fall?
And why would a smaller mortgage need a bigger cut?
Nora borrowed in October 2023, the month the average rate reached 7.79 percent, its highest since 2000.
She wasn't the only one: three in ten of that year's 30-year loans to buy a home carried a rate of 7 percent or more.
Nora isn't a real person; we built her from typical figures, to show how the math works for one household.
She had moved for a new job and bought her first home, with a $375,000 loan at 7.62 percent, the average rate that month.
Every dollar amount in this story is in the dollars of the day, not adjusted for inflation.
You may have heard that a refinance only pays off once rates fall a full percentage point below yours.
We use that one-point line as a test.
And for Nora, rates did cross that line.
In 2026, the average spent 29 weeks at least a full point below her rate.
At the February low, the gap was 1.64 points.
After the week ending July 23, 2026, the gap never again reached a full point, and the window closed.
Whether it opens again, we can't tell you: everything in this story is history, not a forecast, and it covers the US only.
So the letter arrives in a different world.
Say the rate it offers matches the national average for the week ending September 24, 2026: 7.03 percent.
That is just over half a percentage point below what she pays now.
From here on, when we say a point, we mean exactly that: one percentage point off the rate.
Her monthly payment would drop by $221.
Then comes the last line.
$5,124 in loan costs, paid in cash at closing: the lender's charges for making the loan, plus anything she pays up front to buy the rate down.
That bill is not made up.
It is the median from nearly half a million refinances in 2025, and her loan size is the median too.
If a letter like this has reached you, this is the math behind it.
Is it worth paying?
Worth it, here, means one thing: the savings pay back the fees before she sells or moves.
We test a stay of three years, a choice we made, not a rule.
There are two quick answers.
The one-point line says no.
Her cut falls short.
Simple division says yes: $5,124 divided by $221 is about 24 months.
Neither is quite right.
The one-point line never looks at the bill.
And the division leaves something out.
Back to the letter.
Break-even is the month when what she has saved covers the bill.
Division stacks up $221 a month and stops at month 24.
But while the savings stack up, something else is happening, to what she still owes.
Nora has made 35 payments on her old loan, one a month since she moved in.
With each one, a little more goes to paying down what she owes.
A refinance starts a fresh 30-year clock, and in the early years of any mortgage, most of each payment goes to interest.
So the new loan pays down her balance more slowly than the old one would have.
By month 24, she owes $1,133 more than if she had kept the old loan.
If she sold the house then, that $1,133 would come out of her sale money, so it cancels part of what she has saved.
Her savings need a few more months to cover it, and the gap keeps growing a little while they do.
Count it, and break-even moves from month 24 to month 30.
The smaller the cut, the more that gap matters.
Suppose her offer were only a quarter of a point.
Division promises break-even in 38 months.
But a saving that small can't outrun the growing gap in what she owes.
Counting it, the fees never come back, not in all the years her old loan had left.
At a full point, she would break even in 18 months, well inside three years.
Somewhere between a quarter point and a full point is her line.
At half a point, the fees come back in exactly 36 months.
So for Nora, the answer is half a point, half of the one-point line.
Her real offer, just over half a point, clears it.
The February window closed, but by the three-year test, this letter still pays her back.
Is half a point the line for everyone?
Walt is another borrower we built from typical figures.
He bought a smaller house the same month as Nora, with a $115,000 loan at the same rate.
His letter offers the same cut.
The bill at the bottom: $3,667.
His loan is far smaller than hers.
His bill is only a little smaller.
In the 2025 data, refinance bills grow far more slowly than the loans they come with.
For Walt, it comes to about 3.2 percent of what he borrowed.
His savings, though, shrink with the loan.
The same cut takes only $68 a month off his payment.
$68 a month, against a $3,667 bill.
Counting what he owes, he breaks even in month 75.
If Walt sold after three years, he would come out $1,777 behind.
For his fees to come back within three years, his rate would need to fall 1.12 points.
That is more than the one-point line.
So that is why a smaller mortgage needs a bigger rate cut.
The fees barely shrink with the loan, and the monthly savings shrink right along with it.
Anjali sits at the other end.
Same month, same rate, same cut.
She bought a bigger house, with a $655,000 loan, still under the conforming limit: the ceiling for the standard loans that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy.
Her bill is $5,514, close to what Nora pays.
If she sold after three years, she would come out $5,250 ahead.
To get her fees back within three years, she needs a cut of only about a third of a point.
So, back to the letter, and the question it started with.
How big a rate cut makes a refinance worth it, if worth it means the fees come back within three years?
It depends on the size of the loan.
For Walt, with a loan of $115,000, it takes more than a full point.
For Nora, with $375,000, half a point.
For Anjali, with $655,000, about a third of a point.
Put your own loan on that scale, and you can see which of them you are closest to.
Those lines rest on our assumptions: a loan taken out at the October 2023 rate, a median bill, and a three-year stay.
The one-point line fits none of them.
These are national average rates, median bills, and three made-up borrowers.
In 2025, half of refinance bills fell between $3,443 and $8,270.
A real rate, a real bill and a real balance will be different.
Paying the fees by adding them to the loan, or choosing a shorter term, changes the math too.
Which leaves the one number only you know: how long you will stay.
For Nora, the same offer comes out $1,039 ahead if she sells after three years, and $8,093 ahead if she stays seven.
The rate cut decides how fast the fees come back.
How long you stay decides whether you are still there when they do.
