Nina (ILLUSTRATIVE) rents and has $92,000 saved. Her question: should the renter invest the difference? We gave her a pair of twins in every month from January 1991 to July 2016. One buys a home priced at $400,000 (illustrative, the same price in every start month) with 20% down and closing costs, on a 30-year fixed mortgage at that month's average rate. The other rents an equivalent home and puts the same $92,000, plus whatever renting saves each month, into US stocks (total return, dividends reinvested, before fees and taxes). Ten years later we compare the buyer's equity, after the cost of selling, with the renter's pile. All amounts are in dollars of the day.

With the renter's money in cash (last episode), the buyer ended ahead about 3 out of 4 times (75.6%). With it in stocks, the buyer ended ahead in 152 of 307 start months (49.5%): 80 start months switched to the renter, none to the buyer. Along the way, the renter's pile dipped below $92,000 in 183 of 307 windows. Held fifteen years, cash kept the buyer ahead every time; stocks, about 1 in 5 (21.9%). Without the monthly savings invested (only the $92,000), the buyer ended ahead about 2 in 3 times; with a 1% yearly fee, 55.0%. The answer sits on the rent you assume: the split falls near a rent tag of about 5.1% of the price a year, right next to the 5% we assumed; the Census Housing Vacancy Survey's two latest quarters (4.83% and 5.59%) give 47.2% and 58.3%.
