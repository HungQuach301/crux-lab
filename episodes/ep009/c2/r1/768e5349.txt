Over ten years, stocks outgrew home prices about 9 in 10 times. So you'd expect renting and investing to come out ahead of buying a house.

Nina rents, with $92,000 saved and one twin for each choice. With Nina's renting twin holding cash, the buyer ended ahead about 3 in 4 times. With that cash in stocks instead, the buyer led only about half the time.

Over fifteen years, cash kept the buyer ahead every time; stocks, only about 1 in 5. So if Nina rents and invests the difference, does she end up ahead of owning? We replayed the twins in every start month since 1991: stocks outgrew the house almost every time, yet buying still came out ahead in half. It's history, not a forecast. Each start month is a house on one long street, with the same twins inside. The renting twin's pile is the $92,000, plus whatever owning would have cost each month beyond her rent. All of it goes into the whole US stock market, every month, through every drop.

The first house on that street is January 1991. With the renting twin's pile kept in cash, the buyer finished ten years $48,410 ahead. With the pile in stocks, the same buyer finished $485,063 behind. Same house, same mortgage, same rent; the only thing that moved was the renter's pile.

In those ten years, a dollar in the whole US stock market grew almost five times. And the renter's pile was never a single dollar: it was the $92,000 on day one, plus a deposit every month. Whenever owning cost more than renting that month, the gap went into her pile, and the market grew that too. So one change, stocks instead of cash, turned the first house from lit to dark.

A lot of rent-versus-buy math picks one growth rate for stocks and one for homes, then lets them run. That gives a single, tidy answer, and it hides the order in which the good and bad years arrived. Here, every month uses what actually happened: US home prices, rents, mortgage rates, and stock returns. The stock series and its source are on the card.

Everything else is as it was last time on this street. The buying twin's $92,000 becomes the down payment and closing costs on a $400,000 home. She pays the mortgage, property tax, insurance and repairs, and 6 percent to sell when the ten years end. Same costs, same street, one change: the pile now rides the market.

The renting twin's rent comes from one assumption, the tag on her door: a year's rent equal to 5 percent of the price, as of July 2026. Her stocks earn their returns before fees and taxes, with dividends reinvested. And she buys more every single month, even while the pile is falling. So, with the pile in stocks, how much of the street stays lit?

After ten years, a house is lit if the buying twin's equity, after selling costs, is bigger than the renting twin's pile. If the pile is bigger, the door goes dark. That's 307 houses, from January 1991 to the last start with ten full years of data, July 2016. Nina's own month isn't on the street; its ten years are still ahead of her.

Most of the 1990s houses stay lit: 67.6 percent of them. But the darkest house on the whole street is the very first one, January 1991. Its renting twin caught the long stock boom from its first month.

A few years later, the market itself stalled. In 16 start months around March 1999, a dollar in stocks was worth less than a dollar ten years on. Those houses stay lit: the pile lost ground while the buyer kept paying down her loan.

Then the street goes dark, and stays dark. For starts from 2002 through 2007, the buyer ended ahead in none of 72. With cash, 6 of them had stayed lit; with stocks, those lights go out too. These buyers paid near the peak of home prices, while their twins kept buying stocks through the market's fall and rebound.

June 2006 was the darkest house last time, with the pile in cash. With stocks, that buyer ends ten years $261,689 behind. The dark house got darker.

Dark doors don't mean the renting twins had an easy ride. In 183 of the 307 windows, the stock pile dipped below the $92,000 it started with. One renting twin's pile sank to $54,310, and she kept investing. The replay assumes every renting twin did exactly that, every month, through every drop. Real people sometimes stop, and a renter who sells in a crash is not on this street.

So, after the whole walk, how many houses are lit? Lit: 152 of the 307, about half. 80 start months switched from the buyer to the renter, and not one switched the other way. That half is a count of what already happened, not a promise about Nina's month.

April 2012 was the brightest house last time, and it still glows. With stocks, that buyer still ends $237,632 ahead. She bought after prices had fallen, and her house rose on its whole price, not just on her $92,000.

Starts from 2009 through mid-2016 are 78 percent lit. So the half isn't spread evenly: whole stretches are lit, and one long stretch is dark. Those buyers started with lower prices and low mortgage rates, and that held the lights on. Half a street, lit in stretches; now, a longer stay.

Over fifteen years, the street turns over. With cash, every one of the 247 fifteen-year starts left the buyer ahead. With stocks, only about one in five did. The longer the stay, the longer the pile has to compound, and the further it pulls ahead. Longer stays favored the pile; one assumption is still untouched.

So how can stocks outgrow home prices almost every time, and the buyer still finish ahead in half? Because the buyer's house grows on all $400,000 of it, while the renter's pile starts at $92,000. The mortgage lets a slower thing grow on a much bigger base. The renter catches up only through the monthly gap, and through time.

So what if nobody saves that gap, and only the $92,000 is invested? Then the buyer is ahead in about 2 in 3 start months. The monthly habit, not the one big deposit, did most of the renter's catching up.

The pile in this replay also pays no fees and no taxes. With 1 percent a year taken out, the lit share rises to 55 percent. Each real cost on the renter's side moves houses back toward the buyer.

And then there's the tag on Nina's door. At 5 percent, the street splits almost exactly in half. Above about 5.1 percent, most houses light up; below it, most go dark. Public asking-rent data from the Census Bureau put the tag on both sides of that line, and the card shows the range. So the answer sits on the rent tag, and the tag is an assumption.

None of this ranks stocks above a house, or a house above stocks. A home is also stability and control, and no street of numbers counts those.

The rest of the limits are on the card: US only, history, not a forecast, no taxes on gains or on the home, and renters who never skip a month.

So, back to Nina's question. With the pile in cash, most of her street was lit; in stocks, about half; over fifteen years, most of it dark. It's the same street as at the start, read again with the pile in the market. What no house on this street can show is how long Nina herself will stay. Nina's answer: investing the difference made her street a coin flip, and her rent tag tips it.
