You are an American renter in 2026. You have saved enough for a 20% down payment on a home near the national median price. You keep hearing "rent and invest the difference", and you came wondering: if a renter invests the money she doesn't spend on a house, does she really end up ahead of someone who buys?

Over ten years, stocks outgrew homes almost nine times in ten.

So renting looks smart. Nina rents, with $92,000 saved. If her renting twin held cash, her buying twin ended ahead about 3 in 4 times. With that cash in stocks instead, the buyer led only about half the time.

Over fifteen years, cash kept the buyer ahead every time; stocks, only about 1 in 5. So if Nina rents and invests the difference, does she end up ahead of owning? Stocks outgrew the house almost nine times out of ten, yet buying still came out ahead in half. It's history, not a forecast. We replayed every start month since 1991 as a house on one long street: lit if the buyer ends ahead, dark if not. The renting twin's pile is the $92,000, plus whatever owning would have cost each month beyond her rent. All of it goes into the whole US stock market, every month, through every drop.

The first house on that street is January 1991. With the renting twin's pile kept in cash, the buyer finished ten years $48,410 ahead. With the pile in stocks, the same buyer finished $485,063 behind. Same house, same mortgage, same rent; the only thing that moved was the renter's pile.

In those ten years, a dollar in the whole US stock market grew almost five times. And the renter's pile was never a single dollar: it was the $92,000 on day one, plus a deposit every month. Whenever owning cost more than renting that month, the gap went into her pile, and the market grew that too. So one change, stocks instead of cash, turned the first house from lit to dark.

A lot of rent-versus-buy math assumes one growth rate; this replays what actually happened, month by month.

The renting twin's rent is an assumption, the tag on her door: 5 percent of the price a year, as of July 2026, and later, that tag decides. So, with the pile in stocks, how much of the street stays lit?

After ten years, a house is lit if the buying twin's equity, after selling costs, is bigger than the renting twin's pile. If the pile is bigger, the door goes dark. That's 307 houses, from January 1991 to the last start with ten full years of data, July 2016. Nina's own month isn't on the street; its ten years are still ahead of her.

About two in three of the 1990s houses stay lit. But the darkest house on the whole street is the very first one. Its renting twin caught the long stock boom from its first month.

A few years later, the market itself stalled. For a short run of start months, a dollar in stocks was worth less than a dollar ten years on. Those houses stay lit: the pile lost ground while the buyer kept paying down her loan.

Then the street goes dark, and stays dark. For houses bought in the bubble years, the buyer ended ahead in none of them. With cash, some of them had stayed lit; with stocks, those lights go out too. These buyers paid near the peak of home prices, while their twins kept buying stocks through the market's fall and rebound.

Last time, with the pile in cash, the darkest house was one bought near the top. With stocks, that buyer ends more than a quarter of a million dollars behind. The dark house got darker.

Dark doors don't mean the renting twins had an easy ride. Along the way, many of the stock piles dipped below where they started. One renting twin's pile sank to $54,310, and she kept investing. The replay assumes every renting twin did exactly that, every month, through every drop. Real people sometimes stop, and a renter who sells in a crash is not on this street.

So, after the whole walk, how many houses are lit? Lit: 152 of the 307, about half. Every house that changed sides went from the buyer to the renter, never the other way. That half is a count of what already happened, not a promise about Nina's month.

The brightest house last time still glows. With stocks, that buyer still ends well ahead. She bought after prices had fallen, and her house rose on its whole price, not just on her $92,000.

In the years after the crash, most of the lights come back on. So the half isn't spread evenly: whole stretches are lit, and one long stretch is dark. Those buyers started with lower prices and low mortgage rates, and that held the lights on. Over a shorter stay, with the pile in stocks, the buyer was ahead a little more than half the time. In this replay, the longer the stay, the more often the pile finished ahead. Half a street, lit in stretches; now, a longer stay.

Stay longer still, and with stocks, most of the lights go out. With cash, every one of the 247 fifteen-year starts left the buyer ahead. With stocks, only about one in five did. The longer the stay, the longer the pile has to compound, and the further it pulls ahead. Longer stays favored the pile; the rent tag is the one assumption left to test.

So how can stocks outgrow home prices about 9 in 10 times, and the buyer still finish ahead in half? Because the buyer's house grows on all $400,000 of it, while the renter's pile starts at $92,000. The mortgage lets a slower thing grow on a much bigger base. The renter catches up only through the monthly gap, and through time.

So what if nobody saves that gap, and only the $92,000 is invested? Then the buyer is ahead in about 2 in 3 start months. The monthly habit, not the one big deposit, did most of the renter's catching up.

The pile in this replay also pays no fees and no taxes. With 1 percent a year taken out, the lit share rises to 55 percent. Each real cost on the renter's side moves houses back toward the buyer.

And then there's the tag on Nina's door. At 5 percent, the street splits almost exactly in half. The split sits near 5.1 percent, and public rent data from the Census Bureau falls on both sides of it. So the answer sits on the rent tag, and the tag is an assumption.

None of this ranks stocks above a house, or a house above stocks. A home is also stability and control, and no street of numbers counts those.

The rest of the limits are on the card: US only, history, not a forecast, no taxes on gains or on the home, and renters who never skip a month.

So, back to Nina's question. With the pile in cash, most of her street was lit; in stocks, about half; over fifteen years, most of it dark. It's the same street as at the start, read again with the pile in the market. What no house on this street can show is how long Nina herself will stay. Nina's answer: investing the difference made her street a coin flip, and her rent tag tips it.
