# tax-1 claim risk: the $3,000 refund

**Phrasings that would overstate the claim**
- "Your refund costs you $X." The figures are for a model household: $3,000 over-withheld evenly, refund on March 1, national average rates. A real household's cost depends on its own savings yield, card APR and refund date.
- "The government profits from your refund." The measure is only the interest the household gives up. The episode says nothing about government finances.
- "You lose $396 a year if you have card debt." That only holds if each over-withheld dollar would really have paid down a card balance at the average APR. Say "about", and state the assumption on screen.
- Rounding: keep the cents in the script or round consistently ("about $72"). Do not round $7.40 up to "$10".

**What the episode must not say**
- No advice. Do not say "adjust your W-4", "stop over-withholding" or "keep your refund". Present the split (saver vs card borrower) and let the viewer place themselves.
- No forecast. Do not say "with rates where they are, next year it will cost..." Past rates are history, not a forecast.
- Do not imply that under-withholding is free. Under-withholding can trigger penalties, and the episode does not model that.

**Data and rule limits**
- TB3MS is a 3-month T-bill yield used as a proxy for high-yield cash. Many households earn the much lower national savings average (SNDR, from April 2021 only).
- The card rate (TERMCBCCALLNS) is the average across all accounts. It is quarterly and carried forward between observations, and it may be revised.
- Interest on the forgone savings would itself be taxable. The figures are before tax.
- The no-interest rule (refund within 45 days of the deadline) is quoted from 26 U.S.C. 6611(e)(1). Refunds paid later earn interest, and the model ignores that case.
